Information and Innovation:
Evidence From Railroad Expansion in the 19th Century

Author: Shuting Li (CSU San Marcos / Georgia State University)
Discussant Leo Liu
Affiliation University of Technology Sydney

What the Paper Does & Core Findings

  • Research Question: Does infrastructure spur innovation via physical trade/travel or codified technological information (postal mail)?
  • Empirical Setup: Staggered U.S. railroad rollout (1840–1900) carrying Post Office mail routes.
    • Stacked Difference-in-Differences across 24 cohorts (±5-year balanced window).
    • Propensity score matching (1:1 without replacement) on county population, urban share, and white share.
  • Main Findings:
    • Patent volume booms: Railroad arrival increases county patent counts by +11% (OLS) to +23% (Poisson).
    • Quality does not rise: Average novelty and breakthrough importance (Kelly et al. 2021) are flat or negative.
    • "Peripheral convergence": Mail links increase patent similarity specifically toward ordinary peers, not elite hubs.
Central Thesis: Lower communication costs democratized the volume of ordinary invention without improving average quality.

Comment 1: Situating the Contribution in the Literature

  • Building on Established Foundations:
    • Acemoglu et al. (2016, AER): Documented that 19th-century post offices expanded innovation via codified knowledge.
    • Hanlon et al. (2022, NBER): Showed postal rate cuts reduced spatial decay in knowledge diffusion.
  • Reconciling with Prior Railroad Evidence:
    • Perlman (2015, 2016): Found that physical rail access fostered innovation primarily through local agglomeration rather than distant idea diffusion.
    • The Paper's Advance: Moves beyond static rail presence by tracing route-level postal intensity (Trips) and using dense embeddings to capture subtle semantic diffusion that earlier keyword counts missed.
  • Elevating the Paper's Distinctive Conceptual Insights:
    • Peer-to-Peer vs. Hub-and-Spoke: Challenges the classic "superstar city" model by showing communication networks fostered peer diffusion among ordinary, peripheral inventors.
    • Volume vs. Frontier Quality: Reveals that lowering communication costs democratized the volume of incremental invention without necessarily shifting average breakthrough quality.

Comment 2: Physical Track Arrival Is Not a Mail-Information Shock

  • Disentangling Bundled Transportation Shocks: Baseline treatment is physical rail track arrival (Atack 2015). Tracks carry heavy freight, passenger travel, machinists, and buyers all at once.
  • Endogenous Mail Intensity: Weekly trips (Tripsc,t) and routes in Table 4 are contemporaneous post-treatment measures responding to local economic demand.
    • Centrality is frozen at connection year to prevent reverse causality, but mail service intensity is left free to respond to patenting.
  • Potential Solutions:
    • Exploit timing gaps: Compare counties where track opened years before mail contracts were signed to isolate mail from freight.
    • Control for Market Access: Directly include Donaldson & Hornbeck (2016) freight market access in the regression.

Comment 3: What Was in the Mail? Explaining Peripheral Convergence

  • The Paper's Finding (Table 7):
    • Similarity rises +1.26 percentile points between non-hub pairs; zero effect toward urban hubs.
    • Author's takeaway: Horizontal knowledge diffusion among peripheral inventors via rail mail.
  • Plausible Alternative Explanations (What Was in the Mail?):
    • Direct Letters Unlikely: Inventors rarely mailed unpatented, proprietary ideas to distant strangers.
    • Broadcast Print: Subsidized periodicals (Scientific American, USPTO reports) spread common technical vocabulary.
    • Common Problem-Solving: Connected farm counties faced identical bottlenecks (reaper parts, grain elevators), inducing parallel invention.
    • Passenger Mobility: Mail cars ran on passenger trains; higher frequency enabled face-to-face travel and tacit exchange.
    • Potential Extension: Test cross-county co-inventorship (joint patents) to verify collaborative networks.

Comment 4: NLP Methodology: 512-Token Truncation & Contradiction

  • The Stella 512-Token Context Window Limit:
    • Stella (stella_en_1.5B_v5) is trained on 512 tokens (≈ 350 words). 19th-c. patents run 2,000–5,000+ words.
    • Silent truncation discards all technical claims and mechanisms, embedding only introductory boilerplate:
      "Be it known that I, [Name], of [City, County, State]..." + drawing captions ("Fig. 1 is a side view...").
    • Risk: May measure geographic boilerplate overlap rather than technical innovation!
  • Text vs. Appendix Contradiction:
    • Main text claims 1,024-dim Stella embeddings; Appendix A.1 prints formula for TF-IDF / TFBIDF (Eq. 10–12).
    • Potential Solution: Reconcile text and Appendix A.1, document truncation thresholds, and strip legal preambles before embedding.

Other Comments: Natural Experiments & Diagnostics

  • Natural Experiment Specifications:
    • Postal Reforms (1845–1863): Clarifying 11-year cohort window alignment across regimes, and reconciling OLS (+20%) with Poisson (−23.5%) in the 1863 reform.
    • Civil War Mail Suspension: Interpreting Southern post office cuts alongside concurrent wartime disruptions (infrastructure damage, USPTO filing access).
  • Data Reporting & Measurement Diagnostics:
    • Urban Share Summary Stats (Table 1): Reported SD (0.208) exceeds theoretical bound (≈ 0.147) for mean 0.022; check weighting or scaling.
    • Zero Counts & Elasticity: With 61% zeros, log(1 + Patents) underestimates the true extensive-margin response; rely on Poisson estimates.
    • Water Heterogeneity: Ensure control counties are classified by their own water access rather than inheriting the treated county's dummy.

Summary & Concluding Thoughts

  • Core Strengths of the Paper:
    • New Empirical Foundation: Digitizing 38 years of PMG mail contracts linked to rail GIS.
    • Provocative Narrative: Documents peripheral peer-to-peer diffusion using patent text embeddings.
  • Suggestions for Consideration:
    • Avoid Crowding with Information Literature: Many papers sit uncomfortably close in the "information & innovation" space (Acemoglu et al., Hanlon et al., Perlman). This rich PMG-rail setting provides a standout contribution to the infrastructure literature.
    • Pitch Toward "Infrastructure & Innovation": Access to infrastructure is vital for innovation. Pitching toward broad infrastructure access is consistent with all empirical results and avoids the fragile burden of defending a "pure information" channel.
    • Empirical Refinements: Reconcile with Perlman (2015), test timing lags between tracks and contracts, and validate text embeddings on technical claims.
